Build Notes · June 2026

Composting toilets:
they smell less than yours.

The objection: "But won't it smell?" A properly vented composting toilet creates negative pressure at the seat. Air is sucked downward. The bathroom smells better after you use it than before.

Every conversation about composting toilets eventually arrives at the same question. The person asking has never used one. They are imagining a bucket. They are imagining heat. They are imagining the worst toilet they have ever used at a music festival, and mapping that onto the phrase "composting toilet."

The reality is closer to the opposite. A modern composting toilet with urine separation and active ventilation smells less than a conventional flush toilet. Not subjectively. Mechanically. Here is why.

How a flush toilet smells

A conventional toilet bowl is open to the room. When you use it, odours rise directly into the bathroom air. The flush helps, but it also aerosolises particles into the room. The S-bend in the pipe traps sewer gas, but it does not trap the smell generated inside the bowl during use. Extractor fans help after the fact, but they are ceiling-mounted, pulling air from the whole room, not from the source.

The result: the bathroom smells worse immediately after use, then gradually recovers as the extractor runs. Everyone knows this experience. Nobody questions it because it is normal.

How a composting toilet smells

A composting toilet with active ventilation has a small electric fan connected to a vent pipe. The fan runs continuously, pulling air downward through the toilet seat, through the waste chamber, and out through the vent pipe to the exterior. This creates negative pressure at the toilet opening.

When you sit down, the seal between your body and the seat is not perfect, but the airflow is downward. Any odour generated is immediately drawn into the waste chamber and expelled outside. It never enters the room. A Green Building Advisor writer described it precisely: "whenever anyone went to the bathroom and... did their business, the bathroom smelled better than before they went in there." The reason is that the fan was pulling room air into the toilet, not letting toilet air into the room.

Source: "Does a Composting Toilet Stink Up Your House?", Green Building Advisor, 2013.

Urine separation is the other half

The majority of toilet odour comes from anaerobic decomposition, which happens when liquid urine mixes with solid waste in a warm, wet environment. A composting toilet that separates urine from solids at the point of use eliminates this reaction entirely.

The liquid goes to a sealed container or an evaporation chamber. The solids drop into a composting chamber with dry material (sawdust, hemp shavings, or peat) to absorb remaining moisture and begin aerobic decomposition. Aerobic decomposition, with oxygen present, does not produce the sulphurous gases that anaerobic decomposition does. The solids begin to smell like soil or mulch, not sewage.

As one manufacturer puts it: "The compost is typically nearly odorless by itself, thanks to the way excess moisture is separated from the solids."

Source: Waterless Toilet Shop product documentation, 2025.

What you actually need

The minimum viable system for a house is straightforward:

  • A urine-diverting toilet seat or pedestal. Cost: €200–€600 depending on model and material (plastic vs porcelain).
  • A composting chamber below the floor or behind the wall. This can be a commercial unit (€400–€1,200) or a custom-built plywood box with a hinged access door.
  • A 12V or 230V duct fan, 2–5 watts continuous draw. Cost: €30–€80. Annual electricity cost: under €4.
  • A vent pipe, 75–110mm diameter, running vertically to above roof height or horizontally through the wall. PVC pipe and fittings: €20–€50.
  • Bulking agent: sawdust, hemp shavings, or coco coir. Cost: negligible if sourced locally.

Total system cost for a self-built installation: €300–€900. Comparable commercial all-in-one units range from €800–€2,500. For context, a conventional flush toilet plus septic tank or sewer connection in rural Portugal costs €1,500–€4,000 installed, plus ongoing water and sewage fees.

The maintenance reality

A urine container for two people needs emptying every three to seven days. The liquid is sterile, diluted with water, and excellent fertiliser for non-edible plants and trees. The solid chamber, with two people using it full-time, needs partial emptying every three to six months. The material is partially composted, looks and smells like dark soil, and goes into a secondary composting bin to finish for another year before garden use.

This is more hands-on than flushing and forgetting. It is also less hands-on than maintaining a septic tank, which needs pumping every two to five years at €150–€300 per visit, and can fail catastrophically if the drain field clogs.

The cold climate question

In Northern Europe, the concern is freezing. A composting chamber inside the heated envelope of the house, with the vent pipe running through the warm ceiling space before exiting, does not freeze. The composting process itself generates a small amount of heat. In extremely cold climates, an insulated chamber box is sufficient. The system is used year-round in Finland, Norway, and Canada.

The one real constraint: if the compost mass freezes solid and the chamber fills, you cannot empty it until thaw. This is only a problem if the toilet is in an unheated outbuilding used in deep winter. For a primary residence with heating, it is not an issue.

The bottom line

The smell objection is backwards. A well-built composting toilet with urine separation and active ventilation smells less than a flush toilet. The bathroom air is actively pulled through the seat and expelled outside. The waste never sits in water. The room smells like nothing, or faintly like wood shavings, not like a bathroom.

The trade-off is not smell. The trade-off is responsibility. You empty a container every few days instead of flushing into an invisible system you do not maintain. Some people find that unacceptable. Others find it preferable to knowing exactly where their waste goes and what it becomes. There is no universal right answer. There is only an informed one.
